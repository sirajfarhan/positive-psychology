# The Principles

Six principles drawn from Peter Sage's *The Inside Track*, defined by what they
sound like in a sentence rather than by his phrasing. They are **taught and
tracked, never charted**. The three scored dimensions carry the chart. These
carry the teaching.

Each passed three tests. It is **audible** in how someone explains something. It
is **producible** on demand. It is **additive** to permanence, spread and self,
rather than a rename of one of them.

Several favourites failed that filter and are deliberately absent.
*Complaining is the glue* is non-acceptance wearing a different name rather
than a separate skill. *Contrast frames* is the hidden yardstick run in
reverse on purpose, so it already lives inside `compare`. *Identity* is what
permanence scores. And *freedom versus liberty* is an insight worth having
that never surfaces as a language pattern, so there's nothing to drill.

---

## accept: accepting the fact itself

**What it is.** Whether the sentence protests that the thing happened, or states
that it did. This sits *underneath* explanatory style. The three dimensions
score how you explain a cause. This scores whether you have stopped arguing with
the event.

Sage's argument is economic, not moral. Resistance spends energy on a target the
mind and body cannot reach. Acceptance buys two things. The leak stops. That
energy becomes available for the next decision.

He put *what if* and *if only* under zero tolerance on day one.

**Tells.** *what if · if only · I can't believe · should never have · why did
this have to*

**The rung above.** Sage names **pseudo-acceptance** as its own failure.
Accepting begrudgingly, resistance still in it. It drains exactly as much while
feeling responsible. True acceptance is resistance-free.

That discrimination is harder than the first one. It is where most of the
learning is.

| | |
|---|---|
| Low | "I still can't believe they pulled out after all that." |
| Middle | "Fine, they pulled out. Nothing I can do." *(pseudo: note the shrug)* |
| High | "They pulled out. So the question is what I do about the quarter." |

## compare: the hidden yardstick

**What it is.** Almost every complaint carries a silent measurement against a
version that does not exist. The facts are not the problem. The comparison is.

Sage's images: nobody sees a defective adult in a baby, or a failed oak in an
acorn. An empty bank balance is a perfectly empty one.

He runs the skill in reverse too, as **contrast frames**. Meaning is only ever
given in relation to something else. Widen the comparison on purpose and the
same facts mean something different. He cried with relief after breaking his
face skiing, because it could as easily have been his neck.

So this is one skill with two directions: **spot the yardstick you did not
choose, then choose one on purpose.**

**Tells.** *only · just · still · even · barely · by now · should be further
along*

| | |
|---|---|
| Low | "I'm still only doing three a month." *(against an unnamed target)* |
| Middle | "Three a month." |
| High | "Three a month, which is two more than when I had a job as well." |

## agency: where the next move sits

**What it is.** The most load-bearing principle in the book. It is also the one
Seligman's model has no defence against.

Sage's three modes:

**To-Me.** The event acts and you narrate what it did.

**By-Me.** You carve a straight channel through it by force. High-performance,
and still pure resistance. It springs back the moment you stop pushing.

**Through-Me.** You accept the bends and spend your energy on where you sit in
the current. Choose the river, then sail it.

**Why it is not personalization.** Personalization asks *whose fault was the
cause*. Agency asks *whose is the next move*. They come apart completely.

The gap between them is where **bought optimism** lives. A setback explained as
"nothing I could have done" scores as maximally optimistic on the instrument. It
contains no agency at all. That is To-Me wearing optimism's clothes, and only
this concept catches it.

| | |
|---|---|
| To-Me | "They just went quiet on me." |
| By-Me | "I'll chase them every day until they answer." |
| Through-Me | "They've gone quiet. I'll send one close-out note and put the slot back on the market." |

Note what Through-Me keeps: the goal, and a move. What it drops is the grip on
the route.

## question: the question being asked

**What it is.** Sage: questions are the steering wheel of the mind. Your brain
fetches an answer to whatever you ask it, like a dog after a stick, with no
regard for whether the answer helps you. Ask *why does my life suck* and it
returns something dutiful and awful.

The move is noticing which question is running, then replacing it. After his
transfer was cancelled he switched from *why is this happening* to *what does
staying here let me do that leaving wouldn't*. The answer to the second became
the thing he is now known for.

**Tells.** *why does this always · what's wrong with me · why me · why can't I*

| | |
|---|---|
| Low | "Why do I always end up in this position?" *(presupposes a defect)* |
| High | "What did I have control of in that, and what do I do with it next time?" |

The highest form is producing the better question unprompted, before anyone
points out that a question was running.

## weight: how much it was made to matter

**What it is.** Sage's most underrated line: the velcro that all provocations
latch onto is the level of importance attributed to something. Nothing snags on
a surface you have not made sticky.

This is not indifference. It is noticing that the reaction has detached from the
actual stake. Usually the event has been quietly loaded with meaning about you
or your future.

Its far end is his closing principle: train yourself to let go of everything you
fear to lose. Every attachment is an emotional weak spot. Releasing the grip
buys freedom, not numbness.

**Tells.** *this is everything · if this doesn't work then · my whole · make or
break · I need this*

| | |
|---|---|
| Low | "If this one doesn't land, that's the year gone." |
| High | "I'd like this one. It's about eight per cent of the year." |

---

## seed: what it was for

**What it is.** The one Sage row that also appears on the chart, as the `builds`
score. Hill's line, which Sage wrote on a sign above his cell door and read
daily: every adversity carries the seed of an equivalent or greater benefit.
Sage's addition is the operative half. Most people are too busy complaining
about the adversity to water the seed.

It runs wider than the score. His classroom model says your own choices set
which lesson arrives, while how it gets delivered is not up to you. So the
*shape* of what happened points at what you were due. Reading an event as
curriculum rather than as a verdict is the same move as naming what it built.

**The rule that keeps it honest: the cost must survive the sentence.** Naming a
benefit by erasing the loss is denial, not growth, and scores mid at best.

| | |
|---|---|
| Low | "It's just gone." |
| Middle | "Everything happens for a reason." *(cost erased: this is the trap)* |
| High | "It cost me the year and bought me the terms discipline I use on everything now." |

## How these are taught

Identically to the dimensions: the ladder in `learning-loop.md`, with no
technical name before `detect`.

The tells are for **you**, not the learner. At `discriminate`, two of
their sentences sit side by side and one question aims at one of them,
with the other standing as the quiet contrast. For `accept`, name the
settled thing in plain words, then ask what the arguing sentence still
wants: "The deal's dead either way. What's that first sentence still
asking them for?" For `compare`, ask what the number is being measured
against: "Three a month, and your sentence says 'only'. Only next to
what?" Never ask "can you spot the hidden comparison?". And never ask what
each sentence does: a live drill asked exactly that, with a "that" nothing
in the turn resolved, and the learner's whole answer was "do with what?"

The name of the principle arrives only after they have pointed at the thing.

One extra rule for these six. **A principle is only ever drilled on material
already in front of you.** These are not vocabulary. They are ways of hearing
your own sentences, and a drill on a hypothetical teaches the wrong thing.

Where that material lives differs, and the difference matters. `accept`,
`compare` and `seed` are properties of a backward-looking causal statement,
which is exactly what the store holds, so they drill cleanly off a stored
quote. `agency`, `question` and `weight` live in a different kind of sentence:
a stance about what happens next, a question being run, a load being assigned.
The store does not hold those. Drill these three only on something said in the
live conversation, and call `ask` without `--explanation`, so nothing is
mislabelled as the quote under drill.
